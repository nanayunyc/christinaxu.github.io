After Talking to 15 Silicon Valley AI Companies, I See the Open vs. Closed Debate Differently

From Token Optimization to Model Routing: a few of my biggest takeaways from the past month.

~2,800 words 13 min read July 2026 Field research · Menlo Park
Three Conclusions Up Front

Let me start with three conclusions:

Takeaways
01
Silicon Valley's hottest buzzword has shifted from Token Maxing to Value Maxing: enterprises are starting to systematically manage AI costs for the first time.
02
Open-source models are gaining token share faster than most people realize: on one leading coding-agent platform, their share went from 0.1% to 15% in seven months. But token share and revenue share are two very different things.
03
A little over a month ago, Gavin Baker offered a useful framework: frontier models capture 90% of value, open-source models carry 80% of tokens. The latest data suggests the token half is playing out even faster than expected — Chinese models had already surpassed U.S. models in token volume on OpenRouter by early June.

Yesterday, Google reported its Q2 results, which provided a timely footnote to this piece. Google Cloud revenue grew 82% year over year to $24.8 billion, while backlog reached $514 billion. One number was particularly striking: throughput on Google's first-party model APIs had reached roughly 22 billion tokens per minute, up from 16 billion the previous quarter.

We have been talking about Token Optimization for six months, yet total token consumption is still accelerating. This is Jevons Paradox playing out in real time.

And yet the stock sold off after hours. Capex had doubled to $44.9 billion, and Google posted its first quarterly negative free cash flow since going public.

No one is questioning the growth anymore. The market is starting to price the bill.

Growth hasn't slowed. But the logic behind the growth has changed.

Last week, I spent two days at a closed-door gathering in Menlo Park speaking with more than a dozen AI-native companies, including Cursor, Fireworks, Cognition and Sierra. Combined with conversations I've had at Stanford and one-on-one discussions with founders over the past few months, I kept hearing versions of the same questions. And I've become increasingly convinced of one thing:

AI competition is shifting from capability to economics.

For the past two years, the question was who could train the strongest model. Today, enterprises are asking a different question: which tasks actually need the strongest model, and which ones can be handled by something cheaper?

Two concepts capture this shift particularly well: Token Optimization and Model Routing. The first means enterprises are starting to manage AI costs systematically for the first time. The second means companies no longer have to depend on a single model — they can dynamically allocate traffic across closed-source, open-source and even internally developed models depending on the task.

The conversation around open-source models in Silicon Valley has also changed. It is no longer mainly: "Is the model smart enough?" It is increasingly: "Can it handle more production traffic?"

And what sits underneath that question could materially affect the revenue growth trajectories of OpenAI, Anthropic and other frontier labs over the next several years.

I · Token Optimization

Every customer conversation is now about cost

Token Optimization was probably the most frequently discussed topic over those two days. Cursor put it very directly: every customer conversation is about this now.

Companies are redesigning workflows and asking which tasks genuinely require the strongest model, and which can be pushed down to a cheaper one.

But not everyone is hitting the brakes. ClickHouse is an interesting counterexample. They told us their spend on Claude went from roughly $20,000 per month in January to $1 million per month in June — a 50× increase, equivalent to about 5% of their own revenue. Even at that level, the company is still encouraging employees to use AI more, with an expectation that AI spending could rise to 10% of revenue, because they believe the productivity gains are far greater than the cost.

ClickHouse is clearly an outlier. Across the broader group, most companies are already putting on the brakes.

One company described the recent optimization wave as being triggered in part by software vendors collectively moving toward token-based pricing. Microsoft, for example, changed GitHub Copilot to token-based pricing on June 1. Once the cost became visible, companies started managing it.

This reminds me of Cloud Optimization in 2022–2024. Companies did not abandon the cloud; they simply became much more focused on resource utilization and efficiency. But there is something worth remembering: at the time, the bulls also argued that optimization would ultimately lead to more spending. AWS, Snowflake and Datadog subsequently went through four consecutive quarters of growth deceleration.

Will the same pattern repeat this time, with the object of optimization shifting from cloud resources to tokens? That is one of the questions I will be watching most closely in the second half of the year.

And meanwhile, the companies themselves are still growing fast — three examples make the point:

AI spending has not slowed — it has entered a phase of refined operations. Enterprises are still willing to spend, but no longer willing to pay for low-value tokens.

II · Model Routing

Enterprises are no longer buying a single model

If Token Optimization is the goal, Model Routing is the mechanism.

We have already moved beyond the stage where AI simply means giving users a dropdown menu of models. Cognition, for example, can break an inference task into multiple subtasks and assign them to different models based on cost, reasoning ability, latency and accuracy. The user may not even know which model is being called underneath.

What enterprises are really buying is no longer a particular model. They are buying a system that can intelligently coordinate multiple models.

Last year, the question was: who builds the best model? This year, it is: who routes to the best model?

How quickly is this happening? Two data points stood out to me.

Decagon, an AI customer-service company, disclosed that roughly 30% of its token requests are already being routed to open-source models.

On Factory, open-source models represented only 0.1% of token volume last December. That rose to 1% in February and is now around 10–15%. That means the share has increased by more than 100× in seven months. The founder even believes it could reach 90% within 12 months.

I am skeptical of the 90% number. But the direction — and especially the slope — is clear.

And routing to open-source models isn't simply about saving money. Several companies brought up another motivation: they don't want to be locked into any single model provider.

III · The Barbell

Either Great or Cheap. The middle gets squeezed.

After talking to all these companies, I would summarize my view of the model landscape with one word: Barbell.

The model market is likely to develop a barbell structure. Either Great or Cheap. The middle is going to become increasingly difficult to defend.

There will also be consolidation at both ends. At the cheap end, new models have been appearing constantly over the past six months — Kimi K3, GLM 5.2, Qwen and others have taken turns grabbing attention. But the model that continues to set the floor on price-performance is DeepSeek. It remains the largest single model supplier on OpenRouter, ahead of any individual U.S. provider. One investor described it to me as the market's "price-performance floor."

DeepSeek has effectively established a minimum standard for value. Any model that costs more has to be meaningfully better. If it is only marginally better, users may simply use DeepSeek and retry more often. A model that merely matches DeepSeek, or is only slightly better, will have a difficult time surviving.

At the high end, the real question is different: will cheaper models eventually cross the "good enough" line?

The bear case is straightforward. Once a cheap model becomes good enough, most users may simply be satisfied, and the premium commanded by frontier labs could begin to erode.

I don't agree with that view. I don't think demand for frontier intelligence will ever be completely satisfied. Every meaningful step up in model capability unlocks another set of tasks that previously could not be done, or could not be done economically. There will be durable demand at both ends of the barbell.

IV · Tokens vs. Value

Open models are winning tokens faster than value

A little over a month ago, Gavin Baker offered a useful framework:

External · Gavin Baker Framework

"Frontier models capture 90% of the value. Open-source models carry 80% of the tokens."

At the time, I was wondering whether that framework might become outdated quickly. Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4 were still ahead of us. After looking at the latest data, my conclusion is that the token half of the framework is playing out even faster than expected, while the value half is still holding up.

OpenRouter routing data showed that Chinese models had already surpassed U.S. models in token volume by early June. By mid-July, Asian models accounted for roughly 60% of all tokens on the platform, with their share having tripled since the beginning of the year.

Looking back over a full year, the shift is even more striking. Google, OpenAI and Anthropic together went from roughly 70% of token share last June to around 30% this June. DeepSeek alone accounts for more than 16% and has become the largest model supplier on the platform. Its share doubled within six months of the V4 release, with agentic workloads being a major driver.

But the other half of the framework is holding up as well. Anthropic represents only around 12–13% of token volume, yet captures far more revenue than its token share would suggest. In coding-related spending, the Claude family has consistently captured more than 60%.

The market is increasingly separating into two lanes: the commodity lane for volume, and the premium lane for revenue.

There is, of course, an important caveat. OpenRouter's user base is disproportionately made up of developers and price-sensitive traffic, so it does not perfectly represent the enterprise market. But as a directional indicator, the signal is hard to ignore.

A concrete example is Harvey. I spoke with their technical leadership last week. They used proprietary legal data and worked with a third party to perform post-training on an open-source model, then added a router on top. The result was reportedly better than directly using Opus 4.8 — while costing less.

That may be what the future looks like: frontier models remain heavily used, but an increasing share of tokens flows toward task-specific open models.

V · Fully Loaded Cost

List prices are becoming less useful

Another change I find particularly important is how little model list prices — input and output token prices — are actually telling us.

Enterprises don't ultimately care about the price of one million tokens. They care about the total cost of completing the same task.

Cache hit rate, inference stability, retry frequency and token efficiency all affect the final economics. A model that appears to be four times cheaper may only be meaningfully cheaper if it can complete the task with similar reliability. If one model succeeds 60% of the time and requires an average of 1.7 attempts, once retries and cached tokens are included, the apparent price advantage can shrink dramatically.

The feedback I'm hearing from builders in Silicon Valley reinforces this. A cheaper model that needs two or three additional attempts can quickly give back its token-price advantage. And the additional retry traffic can generate cached tokens that further erode the headline savings.

DeepSeek has pushed the price floor extremely low. But one of the most common complaints I hear from production users is not price — it is stability. Outputs can be inconsistent across runs, and the same task may require multiple attempts. A cheaper model that fails more often may not actually be cheaper in production.

What should really be compared isn't the list price per million tokens. It's fully loaded cost per successful task.

The same math applies to cloud vs. on-prem

The same logic applies to the debate over running models in the cloud versus on-prem. There are people on X doing the math around buying hardware to run models locally. For example, GLM 5.2 has been quoted at around $1.40 per million input tokens and $4.40 per million output tokens. A local setup could require roughly $20,000 of hardware while generating around 20 tokens per second. Even assuming the hardware runs 24/7 at full utilization, the break-even period can be roughly 5.5 years.

In an environment where compute is constrained and hardware generations are moving so quickly, that economics increasingly favors the cloud.

This doesn't mean on-prem is irrelevant. There are obviously workloads where data sovereignty, security, latency or extremely high utilization make local infrastructure attractive. But for many enterprises, the cloud still offers a more flexible economic model for rapidly changing inference workloads.

VI · The Availability Constraint

The most underestimated constraint

This is one constraint I think the market underestimates.

Models such as GLM are still heavily constrained by chip availability. They are primarily distributed through inference platforms such as Together and Fireworks, and are not yet broadly available across every major public cloud — although AWS and Azure have started offering some Chinese models. Even Kimi has temporarily paused new-user subscriptions at points because of insufficient compute capacity.

So yes, Chinese models are a threat worth watching. But I think it is still too early to make an aggressive investment call based on token share alone.

The two questions I will keep tracking are:

  1. When will Chinese models achieve large-scale, reliable availability across public clouds?
  2. Is the quality gap versus OpenAI and Anthropic closing, or stabilizing?

The answers to those questions will determine the real impact. Not Twitter sentiment.

VII · Investment Implications

What this means across the stack

Frontier Labs

The question we need to rethink is not whether OpenAI or Anthropic will be replaced by open-source models. It is whether we need to reassess their ARR growth assumptions.

My straightforward view is that some of Anthropic's very strong first-half growth may have come from using the most expensive models for tasks that did not necessarily require frontier-level intelligence. In the second half, some of those workloads will either be eliminated through Token Optimization or routed toward cheaper models.

Expensive models will continue to handle the highest-value tasks. But future revenue growth will increasingly need to come from high-value reasoning, rather than simply having every category of token grow at the same rate.

Semiconductors

I am actually more bullish here. Investors who worry that Token Optimization will suppress GPU demand may be missing Jevons Paradox. Lower unit costs have historically expanded total consumption rather than reducing it. Cheaper models can push AI from early adopters into the mainstream.

And there is another important point: China has a compute shortage. These open-source models ultimately need to be hosted somewhere with sufficient compute capacity.

The center of gravity in the industry will gradually move from training toward inference, but GPUs, HBM, high-speed networking and advanced packaging remain some of the highest-conviction parts of the AI infrastructure stack. One detail I find particularly interesting: giant open models such as Kimi K3 require extremely high HBM density to fit within a rack. The bigger open models become, the happier memory suppliers may be.

Cloud Infrastructure

Cloud providers will increasingly compete on more than GPU availability. The question will be: who can help customers complete inference at the lowest total cost?

Inference efficiency, model deployment, scheduling and TCO will all become more important. The deployment of Chinese models could actually increase rather than reduce demand for cloud compute. Databricks has publicly made a similar point: more models and lower inference costs can create more workloads, not fewer.

AI Software

The biggest opportunity may increasingly sit above the model layer. As models become more commoditized, the platforms that help enterprises automatically select models, optimize costs and improve ROI become more valuable. Routing, inference infrastructure and agent orchestration are all areas worth watching.

This is one reason companies like Fireworks continue to interest me. They are not competing primarily on the model itself. They are competing on the efficiency of the enterprise AI system.

The risk is equally clear. If frontier labs respond to a more competitive model market by vertically integrating further into the software stack, infrastructure software companies could face increasing pressure. That is a competitive dynamic I will be watching closely.

VIII · Bear & Bull Cases

What would make me change my mind?

Bear case · Two points
Cheap models cross the "good enough" threshold and most workloads get diverted away from frontier models.
Token Optimization repeats the 2022–2024 Cloud Optimization playbook. At the time, bulls also argued optimization would ultimately lead to more spending — instead, AWS, Snowflake, Datadog and others experienced several consecutive quarters of growth deceleration.
Bull case · Two points
Jevons Paradox. Lower costs create a much larger market. As the founder of Factory put it: "this cake has rocket boosters strapped to all four sides."
China still doesn't have enough compute. Open models may be capable of generating enormous demand, but their ability to absorb that demand remains constrained by chip supply.

I am personally more tilted toward the bull case. But a thesis should be falsifiable. Over the next two quarters, I will be watching four things:

The four signals
01
Anthropic and OpenAI revenue signals. If some of the first-half growth really did come from using the most expensive models for lower-value workloads, the first sign should appear in the sequential growth rate. Third-party estimates circulating on X already suggest Anthropic ARR continues to reach new highs, but its growth rate is beginning to flatten; the same tracker suggests OpenAI may actually be accelerating. The debate over those curves is arriving sooner than I expected.
02
Availability of Chinese models. Can they move beyond Together and Fireworks and into large-scale, reliable public-cloud distribution? That is a prerequisite for turning token share into revenue share.
03
The direction of the quality gap. I care less about benchmarks than I do about what builders experience. Retry rate is more informative to me than a benchmark score.
04
Whether fully loaded cost becomes the language of procurement. If it does, many of today's claims about a model being "5× cheaper" will have to be recalculated.
Closing

The competition function has changed

AI competition isn't over. The function being optimized has changed.

In the past, the industry optimized for Intelligence. Increasingly, it will optimize for Intelligence per Dollar.

What we optimized was Intelligence. What we'll optimize is Intelligence per Dollar.

I'll come back to this in 12 months and see where the thesis held — and where it didn't.
